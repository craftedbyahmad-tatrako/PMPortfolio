Project Governance Framework

Built a project management system from zero: ten practical tools every project now runs on, grounded in PMBOK® practice.
Project
Project Governance Framework
Context
Food manufacturing, growing from 2 to 7 lines
My role
Associate Project Manager
Tools
Excel, SharePoint, Smartsheet, MS Project, Primavera P6
Result
5 projects on one system
01

Overview and problem

What it was
A full project management framework designed from scratch for a company that had none, covering how every project is started, planned, run, controlled and closed.
The problem
Projects ran without a shared structure for scope, schedule, cost, risk or change, while the plant was growing from 2 to 7 lines.
02

Goals and success criteria

Structure
One repeatable way to run every project.
Control
Every change, risk and variance on record.
Adoption
Engineers and sponsors use it willingly.
Scalability
Works for small and large projects.
03

My role and the team

I owned
The whole framework: design, templates, procedures, roll-out, coaching and improvement, applied on all five capital projects.
I contributed to
Reporting formats, shaped with sponsors and operations.
Others handled
Project work by engineers and technicians, who followed the procedures.
Stakeholders
Sponsors, operations, engineers, technicians, vendors, quality team.

Mixed experience across the team.
A framework had to stay light to be used.

06

The framework in practice

Ten practices every project runs on, each with a rule people follow and a project where it paid off.
01
Project charter
One page: why, scope, budget ceiling and sponsor sign-off.
The rule: No work starts without a signed charter.
Seen on: Cheese plant: capacity target and budget ceiling set first.
02
Stakeholder map
Who matters, their power and interest, and who needs which update.
The rule: Every stakeholder has an owner and a rhythm.
Seen on: Mayonnaise line: sales and operations updated weekly.
03
WBS standard
The project broken into work packages with one owner each.
The rule: Every deliverable appears once and has one owner.
Seen on: Cheese plant: about 30 work packages.
04
Three baselines
Approved scope, schedule and cost.
The rule: Nothing is ordered before the baselines are signed.
Seen on: All five capital projects.
05
Risk register
Top risks with an owner, a trigger and a response.
The rule: Reviewed in every status meeting.
Seen on: Shortening line: the valve delay was already on the register.
06
Procurement plan
Which items are long-lead, who supplies them and when they are due.
The rule: Long-lead items are ordered first.
Seen on: Cheese plant: longest-lead equipment ordered first.
07
Change control (CCB)
A written request with its cost and time impact, decided by a board.
The rule: No change is built without approval.
Seen on: Shortening line: re-sequencing approved through the CCB.
08
Status and variance report
One page: progress, variance against baselines and forecast.
The rule: Same format on every project.
Seen on: Sauces line: tracked against the 20% target.
09
Stage gates
Go/no-go checks before installation and before handover.
The rule: A gate must be passed to move on.
Seen on: Cheese plant: “ready to install” check.
10
Handover and lessons
Acceptance, a handover pack and a lessons-learned log.
The rule: Lessons feed the next project’s plan.
Seen on: Sauces line: lessons set changeover targets.

07

Key decision

The situation
A full textbook system would have buried a busy team in paperwork.
What I did
Built on PMBOK® but kept only the ten practices that change project outcomes, scaled to project size.
Options and trade-offs
(1) Apply every PMBOK process: complete, but heavy. (2) A minimal checklist: light, but gaps appear later. (3) Ten core practices scaled to project size. I chose (3) so nothing important was missed and small projects stayed light.
